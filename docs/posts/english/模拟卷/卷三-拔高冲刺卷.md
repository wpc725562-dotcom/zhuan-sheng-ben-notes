**The Psychology of Procrastination**

Procrastination — the act of delaying tasks despite knowing the negative consequences — is a phenomenon that affects approximately 20% of the adult population worldwide. While it is often dismissed as laziness, research in cognitive psychology has revealed that procrastination is far more complex than simple laziness.

Dr. Timothy Pychyl, a psychology professor at Carleton University, defines procrastination as "the voluntary delay of an intended course of action despite expecting to be worse off for the delay." This definition highlights two critical elements: first, the delay is voluntary, meaning the person chooses to delay; second, the person is aware that the delay will likely lead to negative outcomes. This distinguishes procrastination from other forms of delay, such as strategic delay, where one deliberately postpones a task to achieve a better outcome.

The root cause of procrastination, according to recent studies, is not poor time management but rather emotional regulation. When faced with a task that triggers negative feelings — such as anxiety, boredom, or insecurity — the brain seeks immediate relief by turning to more pleasurable activities. This is known as "mood repair." The problem is that while mood repair provides temporary relief, it ultimately increases stress as deadlines approach.

Interestingly, a study published in the Journal of Consumer Psychology found that forgiveness is a more effective strategy for overcoming procrastination than self-criticism. Students who forgave themselves after procrastinating on a first exam were more likely to study effectively for the next exam, compared to those who blamed themselves. According to the lead researcher, "Self-forgiveness allows individuals to move past their previous mistakes and focus on future performance."

Understanding procrastination as an emotional regulation issue rather than a character flaw can help us approach it with more compassion and develop more effective strategies to overcome it.

### Passage C: 长难句密集

**The Paradox of Choice**

In his book *The Paradox of Choice*, psychologist Barry Schwartz argues that while some choice is undoubtedly better than none, having too many choices can lead to anxiety, indecision, and dissatisfaction. This phenomenon, which Schwartz terms "the paradox of choice," challenges the widely held assumption that more choice necessarily leads to greater well-being.

Schwartz's research suggests that when people are presented with a limited set of options — for example, six instead of thirty — they tend to make decisions more quickly and feel more satisfied with their choices. Conversely, when faced with an overwhelming array of options, individuals may experience what is known as "analysis paralysis," a state in which the fear of making the wrong choice prevents them from making any choice at all.

A classic experiment conducted by psychologists Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper illustrates this point. In a supermarket, shoppers were offered the opportunity to taste jams from either a display of 6 varieties or a display of 24 varieties. The results were striking: while the larger display attracted more initial interest, shoppers who saw the smaller display were ten times more likely to make a purchase. The researchers concluded that having too many options can actually paralyze decision-making.

Schwartz identifies two distinct types of decision-makers: "maximizers" and "satisficers." Maximizers are individuals who seek the best possible option, exhaustively comparing all available alternatives. Satisficers, on the other hand, settle for an option that is "good enough" and meets their criteria. The research shows that maximizers consistently report lower levels of happiness and higher levels of regret than satisficers, even when they make objectively better decisions.

The implications of the paradox of choice are significant for both consumers and policymakers. By understanding the cognitive limits of human decision-making, we can design environments that facilitate rather than hinder choice. As Schwartz puts it, "Learning to choose is hard. Learning to choose well is harder. But learning to choose well in a world of unlimited choice is even harder."
